ABSTRACT
Retailers must maintain enough stock to satisfy demand without accumulating unnecessary inventory. Demand variability, seasonality, promotions and incomplete information can contribute to stockouts, excess stock and inefficient use of working capital. This project proposes a Smart Inventory Demand Forecasting and Optimization System that uses historical retail sales data to forecast product demand and support replenishment decisions. The general objective is to develop a machine-learning-based system that improves retail stock decision support. The specific objectives are to analyze historical sales patterns, compare selected forecasting models, develop an inventory decision rule for identifying stock risks and recommending replenishment quantities, and evaluate the resulting approach.
The study will use an applied research design combining requirements elicitation from retail personnel with experimental evaluation using secondary historical sales data. Purposive sampling will be used to recruit approximately 20–30 personnel with direct inventory, sales or procurement responsibilities, subject to supervisor approval and access. A structured questionnaire and a short semi-structured interview guide will support requirements analysis. The forecasting experiment will clean and transform the dataset, construct time-safe features, and compare a baseline with selected machine-learning models using chronological train and holdout periods. MAE and RMSE will be reported, with MAPE used only where actual demand is non-zero and interpretation is meaningful. Forecasts will be combined with current and in-transit stock and explicit replenishment assumptions to produce explainable stock-risk indicators and suggested order quantities. The expected output is a web-based decision-support prototype; it will not automatically place purchase orders. Actual findings will be reported only after implementation and evaluation.
Keywords: retail inventory, demand forecasting, machine learning, replenishment, stockout risk, decision support.

DEFINITION OF TERMS
Demand forecasting: Estimating the quantity of a product likely to be demanded over a future period using historical and other relevant information.
Inventory position: A decision-support quantity defined for this study as on-hand stock plus confirmed in-transit stock, less allocated stock where allocation data are available.
Inventory optimization: Selecting replenishment quantities or stock targets that balance product availability against unnecessary stock, subject to available data and stated assumptions.
Machine learning: Computational methods that learn patterns from data and use those patterns to generate predictions or support decisions.
Overstock: Stock materially above a defined target or expected requirement for the selected horizon; the threshold will be configured and disclosed.
Replenishment: Restoring inventory by ordering or transferring stock to meet expected requirements.
Stockout: A condition in which available inventory is insufficient to satisfy demand.
Time-aware evaluation: A model-evaluation approach that preserves chronological order so that future observations do not leak into training.

CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Statement of the Problem
Retail inventory decisions require a balance between product availability and the cost of holding unnecessary stock. Product demand can change with trends, seasonality, promotions and other market conditions. Underestimating demand may leave a product unavailable when customers need it; overestimating demand may tie up working capital and increase storage or obsolescence exposure. The operational challenge is therefore not simply to record the stock on hand, but to anticipate demand and translate that estimate into a timely, defensible replenishment decision.
Historical sales data can reveal recurring demand patterns, while current inventory and replenishment context determine whether forecast demand can be met. Liu et al. (2025), using data from a retailer with more than 1.6 million SKUs, report that current inventory, near-term forecast demand and recent sales were influential in stockout prediction. Their study illustrates why prediction should incorporate inventory context rather than treating future demand in isolation. Bergsma, de Ruijt and Bhulai (2025) review 122 studies and distinguish approaches that estimate demand before a separate optimization step from more integrated or dynamic methods. Their review notes that important operating conditions and real-world validation remain unevenly addressed.
The research problem addressed by this project is the difficulty of predicting changing product demand and converting predicted demand into practical replenishment support. A conventional stock record describes present quantities but does not estimate future requirements. Conversely, a demand model may generate forecasts without determining whether available stock is adequate or what quantity should be considered for replenishment. The proposed study will investigate selected forecasting models and connect the chosen forecast to a transparent inventory decision rule, evaluated as a decision-support prototype.
1.2 Proposed solution
The project will analyze historical retail sales, construct time-safe forecasting features, and empirically compare a simple baseline with selected machine-learning models. The final model will be selected on the basis of held-out forecasting evidence and practical feasibility, not assumed in advance. Forecasts will then be combined with product-level inventory position and configurable assumptions such as lead time and safety stock. The system will present the forecast horizon, stock-risk status and suggested replenishment quantity with its underlying assumptions so that an authorized inventory user retains the final decision.
1.3 Objectives
1.3.1 General objective
To develop a machine-learning-based inventory demand forecasting and optimization system for improving retail stock replenishment decisions.
1.3.2 Specific objectives
1. Analyze historical retail sales data and identify patterns and factors associated with product demand.
2. Evaluate selected machine-learning models for forecasting future retail product demand.
3. Develop an inventory decision approach that uses forecast demand to identify stockout and overstock risks and recommend replenishment quantities.
4. Evaluate the forecasting accuracy and decision-support effectiveness of the proposed system.
1.4 Research questions
1. What patterns and factors in historical retail sales data are associated with product demand?
2. How do selected machine-learning models compare in forecasting future retail product demand?
3. How can forecast demand be combined with inventory information to identify stockout and overstock risks and determine replenishment quantities?
4. To what extent does the proposed approach provide accurate forecasts and support retail replenishment decisions?
1.5 Justification and significance
The project is academically justified by the continuing need to connect predictive performance with inventory decisions. Comparing a baseline and selected forecasting models will provide empirical evidence on a defined dataset, while the integrated decision logic will demonstrate how forecasts can inform stock-risk assessment. The project is practically relevant because inventory personnel may benefit from seeing expected demand alongside current stock and a transparent replenishment suggestion. The application will support—not replace—human judgement. It also provides an applied integration of data analysis, machine learning, relational databases and web application development. Because the planned experimental data may be international, the results will not be represented as proof of performance across Kenyan retailers.
1.6 Scope and Delimitations
The study covers product-level historical demand analysis, comparison of a limited set of forecasting models, a prototype for inventory and sales records, forecast-driven stock-risk indicators, replenishment suggestions, and software and usability evaluation. Requirements will be elicited from selected retail personnel where access and permission are obtained. Machine-learning evaluation will use a public secondary retail dataset unless a suitable authorized dataset becomes available. The study will not build a full enterprise resource-planning platform, optimize supplier networks, make automatic purchases, or claim live commercial deployment. For this project, “optimization” is operationalized as a transparent forecast-driven target-stock and reorder rule, not a claim of globally optimal supply-chain decisions. The final dataset, forecast horizon, model candidates and system stack will be documented after confirmation during implementation.

CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Introduction
This chapter reviews theoretical and empirical literature related to machine learning demand forecasting and inventory optimization in retail environments. The review focuses on retail inventory management, demand forecasting, machine learning approaches for predicting product demand, stockout and overstock risk, and inventory replenishment optimization. Recent studies are examined to establish the techniques that have been applied by other researchers, the methodologies adopted, their findings and the limitations that remain. Particular attention is given to the relationship between forecasting accuracy and inventory decision-making because accurate prediction alone may not necessarily result in effective inventory management. The chapter further presents the conceptual framework, provides a critique and summary of the reviewed literature, and identifies the research gap that the proposed study intends to address.
2.2 Theoretical Review and Conceptual Framework
2.2.1 Retail Inventory Management
Inventory management involves planning and controlling the quantities of goods maintained by an organization to ensure that products are available when required while avoiding unnecessary accumulation of stock. Within retail businesses, inventory decisions are particularly important because retailers manage numerous products whose demand may change over time. Insufficient inventory can result in stockouts and lost sales, while excessive inventory can increase holding requirements and tie up financial resources. Effective inventory management therefore requires decisions concerning which products should be replenished, when replenishment should occur and the quantities that should be ordered. These decisions depend significantly on information concerning current inventory, historical sales, expected future demand and other operational factors.
Bergsma, de Ruijt and Bhulai (2025) reviewed 122 studies involving machine learning in inventory control and identified different approaches through which predictive techniques have been connected to inventory optimization. Their review demonstrates that machine learning can support inventory management by estimating uncertain variables such as demand before those estimates are incorporated into inventory decisions. Liu et al. (2025) similarly demonstrate the importance of current inventory, recent sales and short-term demand forecasts in predicting retail stockouts. Seyedan, Mafakheri and Wang (2023) combine time-series demand forecasting with an order-up-to-level inventory policy and show how forecast distributions can be translated into safety stock and replenishment levels. Together, these studies indicate that inventory decisions can benefit from combining historical information, current inventory conditions and predictions of future demand.
The relevance of improved inventory management is also evident within the Kenyan retail environment. Mwove and Kithandi (2026a) examine AI-based inventory management among large supermarkets in Nairobi City County and identify stockouts, overstocking, unpredictable demand and increasing operational costs among supermarket supply-chain challenges. Mwove and Kithandi (2026b) separately examine AI demand forecasting and supply-chain performance among large supermarkets in Nairobi City County, demonstrating local interest in predictive inventory technologies. Muteti and Makhamara (2026) examine digital inventory management at Naivas Supermarkets and report a positive relationship between digital inventory practices and supermarket performance. These studies provide regional evidence supporting continued investigation of data-driven inventory-management approaches, although their emphasis is organizational rather than the technical development and experimental evaluation of forecasting models.
2.2.2 Retail Demand Forecasting
Demand forecasting refers to the process of estimating the quantity of a product that customers are expected to demand during a future period. Within retail environments, demand forecasting supports decisions concerning purchasing, inventory replenishment, resource planning and product availability. Historical sales data provide an important source of forecasting information because they can reveal trends, seasonal variations and other recurring demand patterns. However, retail forecasting is challenging because products can exhibit substantially different sales behaviours, including stable, seasonal, irregular and intermittent demand. Consequently, the forecasting technique used for a particular retail problem should be evaluated empirically rather than assuming that a single technique will perform equally well across all products.
Makridakis, Spiliotis and Assimakopoulos (2022) demonstrate the scale and complexity of retail forecasting through the M5 forecasting competition, which used Walmart retail sales information and required forecasting across multiple hierarchical levels. The competition involved thousands of products and a large number of related retail time series, providing an important benchmark for evaluating statistical and machine learning forecasting techniques. The results demonstrate the importance of evaluating forecasting methods on realistic retail data rather than relying exclusively on theoretical assumptions. The M5 data also contain historical sales, calendar characteristics and price information that can be used in feature-based forecasting experiments. The dataset is therefore suitable as a secondary experimental dataset for the proposed research, while its United States retail origin will be acknowledged as a limitation when interpreting results for Kenyan retail settings.
Mejía and Aguilar (2024) address the diversity of product-demand behaviour by proposing a retail demand forecasting approach combining time-series feature engineering, product characterization and ensemble learning. Andrade and Cunha (2023) similarly investigate disaggregated retail demand forecasting using gradient boosting and demonstrate that machine learning can model complex store-SKU demand while maintaining a high level of automation. Ma (2024) extend the problem by considering cross-SKU promotional interactions through an attributed-space graph recurrent neural network for store-SKU replenishment planning. These studies show that forecasting performance may depend on the structure of the data, available explanatory features and relationships among products. The proposed research therefore begins with exploratory analysis and feature engineering before comparing selected models.
2.2.3 Machine Learning for Demand Forecasting
Machine learning provides computational techniques that can identify relationships within historical data and use the identified patterns to generate predictions for previously unseen observations. In retail demand forecasting, machine learning can process historical sales together with other available variables to estimate future demand. Unlike simple fixed forecasting rules, machine learning models can capture nonlinear relationships when sufficient and appropriate data are available. However, model complexity alone does not guarantee better forecasts, making objective model evaluation necessary. The proposed research will therefore evaluate selected machine learning models rather than predetermining that a particular algorithm will provide the most suitable solution.
Andrade and Cunha (2023) investigate gradient boosting for disaggregated retail forecasting, while Mejía and Aguilar (2024) combine feature engineering with ensemble learning for product-category demand forecasting. Ma (2024) use recurrent and graph neural-network components to capture demand history and cross-item promotional interactions. These approaches illustrate a progression from conventional supervised learning toward more complex architectures designed to capture temporal and relational information. Nevertheless, advanced models can introduce additional computational requirements and may be less interpretable. For the proposed project, model selection will therefore be based on empirical forecasting performance, computational practicality and suitability for the available data.
Van der Haar et al. (2024) demonstrate a different integration strategy by using supervised learning with custom loss functions to directly predict inventory order sizes rather than first predicting demand. Their work considers feature information such as promotions and trends and shows that machine learning can be integrated more closely with inventory control. Bergsma, de Ruijt and Bhulai (2025) similarly classify machine-learning inventory research into separate estimation and optimization, static integrated optimization and dynamic approaches. The review also identifies challenges involving scalability, stochastic lead times, large action spaces, product interactions and multi-echelon systems. These findings support selecting an approach whose complexity corresponds to the research problem and available resources.
2.2.4 Stockout and Overstock Risk
A stockout occurs when available inventory is insufficient to satisfy product demand, whereas overstock occurs when inventory substantially exceeds expected requirements. Both situations are undesirable because stockouts can result in missed sales and reduced product availability, while overstocking can increase storage requirements and tie up financial resources. Demand uncertainty makes these conditions difficult to prevent because future product requirements are not known with complete certainty. Demand forecasts can reduce this uncertainty by providing an estimate of expected product requirements during future periods. Nevertheless, forecast information needs to be considered alongside available inventory before a potential stock problem can be identified.
Liu et al. (2025) develop a machine learning approach for inventory stockout prediction using a retailer dataset containing more than 1.6 million SKUs. Their analysis identifies current inventory, short-term demand forecasts and recent sales as particularly influential variables in predicting stockouts. Ali et al. (2024) investigate machine learning for anticipating customer backorders and show that simplified models using a smaller number of high-impact predictors can substantially reduce computational cost with only limited reductions in predictive performance. These findings are important because they suggest that useful inventory-risk prediction does not necessarily require an excessively large number of variables. The proposed project will therefore focus on demand and inventory variables that are supported by literature and available within the selected data sources.
The Kenyan retail context also provides evidence of the relevance of stockout reduction and improved inventory visibility. Mwove and Kithandi (2026a; 2026b) identify stockouts, overstocking and unpredictable demand as continuing supermarket supply-chain concerns while examining AI-based inventory and forecasting practices. Muteti and Makhamara (2026) similarly discuss digital inventory management in relation to supermarket performance. These studies establish contextual relevance but do not experimentally compare forecasting models or implement the stock-risk logic proposed in this project. The present study therefore uses their findings as contextual support rather than as substitutes for technical model evaluation.
2.2.5 Inventory Optimization and Replenishment
Inventory optimization involves determining inventory decisions that balance product availability with the costs and risks associated with holding stock. Replenishment represents an important component of inventory optimization because businesses need to determine when additional stock is required and how much should be obtained. Forecasted demand can support this decision by providing an estimate of the quantity likely to be required during a future period. However, replenishment decisions also depend on available stock and other inventory parameters and therefore cannot be based exclusively on predicted sales. An effective decision-support approach should consequently connect forecasting results with inventory conditions.
Seyedan, Mafakheri and Wang (2023) propose an order-up-to-level inventory optimization model that combines ensemble deep learning demand forecasts with safety stock, reorder-point and order-quantity calculations. Van der Haar et al. (2024) present an integrated supervised-learning approach that predicts optimal order sizes directly for multi-period inventory problems. Ma (2024) connect store-SKU forecasting with replenishment planning and report improvements in both forecasting loss and inventory cost in their empirical experiments. These studies demonstrate that forecasting becomes more practically valuable when its outputs contribute directly to inventory decisions. They also demonstrate that there are different levels of complexity through which forecasting and replenishment can be integrated.
For the proposed research, the separate forecasting and optimization approach is appropriate because it enables the forecasting model and inventory decision component to be evaluated independently. Forecasting performance can first be measured using quantitative prediction-error metrics, after which the selected forecasts can be incorporated into stock-risk assessment and replenishment calculations. This separation improves interpretability because forecasting errors can be distinguished from errors in inventory decision logic. It also reduces implementation complexity compared with reinforcement-learning or highly integrated deep-learning inventory policies while retaining a meaningful machine-learning research component. The final replenishment recommendation will serve as decision-support information rather than automatically replacing the judgement of inventory personnel.
2.2.6 Machine Learning and Inventory Management in the Kenyan Retail Context
Recent Kenyan research demonstrates increasing interest in artificial intelligence and digital inventory management within the retail sector. Mwove and Kithandi (2026b) investigate AI demand forecasting among large supermarkets in Nairobi City County and focus on the relationship between forecasting practices and supply-chain performance. Their work provides evidence that AI-based forecasting is relevant within the Kenyan supermarket environment, although its emphasis is organizational rather than experimental comparison of forecasting algorithms. Mwove and Kithandi (2026a) separately examine AI-based inventory management and identify inventory challenges including stockouts, overstocking and unpredictable demand. These studies provide important local justification for the proposed research while leaving room for a technical Computer Science contribution.
Muteti and Makhamara (2026) investigate digital inventory management and performance at Naivas Supermarkets in Nairobi City County using employee responses and statistical analysis. Their study supports the importance of digital inventory practices but does not develop or experimentally evaluate a machine-learning forecasting component. The proposed project differs by using historical retail time-series data to compare forecasting models and by implementing the selected model within a web-based inventory decision-support system. Requirements data from retail personnel will be used to understand practical workflows and system needs, while the machine-learning experiment will use historical sales observations. This separation prevents organizational questionnaire responses from being treated as forecasting training data and provides a clearer methodological structure.

2.3 Critique of Existing Literature
The reviewed literature demonstrates considerable progress in applying machine learning to demand forecasting and inventory management but also reveals differences in research objectives and levels of technical complexity. Studies such as Andrade and Cunha (2023) and Mejía and Aguilar (2024) concentrate primarily on improving demand forecasting through gradient boosting, feature engineering and ensemble methods. Such research provides useful evidence regarding forecasting techniques but does not necessarily establish whether improved prediction accuracy produces appropriate replenishment decisions. Conversely, approaches such as Ma (2024) and van der Haar et al. (2024) connect machine learning more closely to inventory decisions but introduce greater modelling and implementation complexity. This suggests a need to select an approach that balances forecasting performance, interpretability and implementation feasibility.
Another limitation concerns the distinction between prediction accuracy and operational usefulness. Liu et al. (2025) demonstrate that stockout prediction benefits from combining inventory information, short-term demand forecasts and recent sales rather than relying on historical sales alone. Bergsma, de Ruijt and Bhulai (2025) similarly show that machine learning can contribute to inventory control at several stages and identify continuing challenges in applying advanced approaches to realistic inventory settings. These findings indicate that a forecasting model should not be evaluated solely as an isolated predictive algorithm when its intended purpose is inventory management. The proposed research addresses this concern by evaluating the forecasting component quantitatively and then examining how its outputs can be incorporated into stock-risk and replenishment decisions.
The Kenyan studies reviewed provide contextual evidence but have a different emphasis from the proposed Computer Science research. Mwove and Kithandi (2026a; 2026b) and Muteti and Makhamara (2026) examine relationships between AI or digital inventory practices and organizational performance using survey-oriented methodologies. These studies demonstrate that intelligent inventory management is relevant within Kenya but do not experimentally compare machine learning forecasting models using historical retail time-series data. They also do not provide the type of integrated software implementation proposed in this study, where forecasts are translated into stock-risk information and replenishment recommendations. The proposed research therefore builds on their contextual findings from a computational and system-development perspective.
2.4 Summary
The literature reviewed in this chapter demonstrates that inventory management, demand forecasting and replenishment decisions are closely related. Historical retail sales data contain information that can be analyzed to identify demand patterns, while machine learning provides techniques capable of learning from these patterns and predicting future product requirements. Recent studies have investigated gradient boosting, ensemble learning, deep learning and integrated supervised learning for retail forecasting and inventory control. Consequently, model selection should be based on empirical evaluation using appropriate data and performance measures rather than assuming that a particular algorithm will provide the best solution. The reviewed evidence also supports combining forecasted demand with current inventory information when assessing stock risks and replenishment requirements.
The literature further demonstrates that demand forecasts become more useful for inventory management when they are connected to operational information and inventory policies. Liu et al. (2025) show the importance of current inventory, recent sales and short-term forecasts in stockout prediction, while Bergsma, de Ruijt and Bhulai (2025) identify several approaches through which machine learning can be integrated with inventory optimization. Kenyan studies demonstrate the local relevance of AI demand forecasting and digital inventory management but primarily address organizational outcomes. The proposed study therefore adopts a technical perspective that experimentally evaluates selected forecasting models and integrates the selected model with inventory decision support. This approach provides a clear connection between the literature, research questions and proposed implementation.
2.5 Research Gaps
Existing literature provides substantial evidence concerning machine learning demand forecasting, stockout prediction and inventory optimization, but a gap remains in the practical connection between these areas within an accessible inventory decision-support system. Several forecasting studies concentrate on improving predictive accuracy through advanced models, feature engineering or ensemble methods, whereas inventory-oriented studies may use more complex integrated optimization and deep-learning approaches. Bergsma, de Ruijt and Bhulai (2025) identify continuing challenges in translating machine learning research into realistic inventory-control environments. These findings indicate that there remains value in investigating a transparent and measurable approach in which forecasting and inventory decision support can be independently evaluated and subsequently integrated. Such an approach is especially appropriate where implementation feasibility and interpretability are important.
A second gap concerns the relationship between forecasting performance and inventory outcomes. An algorithm that produces a lower prediction error does not automatically demonstrate that its forecasts will produce useful inventory decisions. Liu et al. (2025) show that stockout prediction depends strongly on combining demand-related information with current inventory, while Seyedan, Mafakheri and Wang (2023) demonstrate how forecast distributions can be translated into replenishment variables. Consequently, there is a need to investigate how forecasts generated from historical retail sales can be translated into understandable stockout and overstock indicators and replenishment recommendations. The proposed research addresses this gap by extending model evaluation beyond prediction accuracy to the subsequent inventory decision-support process.
A contextual and methodological gap is also evident within the Kenyan literature. Recent Kenyan studies establish relationships between AI demand forecasting, digital inventory management and supermarket performance, but their methodologies primarily rely on questionnaires and organizational statistical analysis. They provide important evidence concerning the relevance of intelligent inventory technologies but do not experimentally compare selected machine learning forecasting algorithms using historical retail sales time series and integrate the resulting model into an operational inventory decision-support application. The proposed research will therefore contribute a technical perspective by evaluating selected machine learning models and implementing a mechanism that translates forecasts into stockout, overstock and replenishment information. This represents a specific and defensible contribution without claiming that AI inventory management has not previously been researched in Kenya.

CHAPTER 3: SYSTEM ANALYSIS AND DESIGN
3.1 Introduction
This chapter specifies how requirements will be elicited, how data will be analyzed, and how the web-based system and forecasting experiment will be designed. Requirements research and model training use distinct information sources: personnel responses inform user and workflow needs, while historical sales records support model development.
3.2 Research and development design
The study will use an applied research design with two complementary strands. First, requirements elicitation will use questionnaires and, where feasible, interviews with retail personnel. Second, an experimental strand will compare candidate forecasting methods on secondary historical retail data using chronological partitions. Findings from both strands will inform a prototype and its evaluation. The proposal will be updated if supervisor guidance or data access requires a material change.
3.3 Systems development methodology
An iterative Agile approach is proposed. Requirements and system models will be refined before development; implementation will then proceed through working increments: core data and authentication; product, sales and inventory functions; forecasting experiments and service; stock-risk and replenishment logic; dashboard integration; and final evaluation. Each increment will be reviewed against requirements and tested before integration. Iteration is appropriate because the final forecast model depends on empirical performance rather than a predetermined algorithm.
3.4 Feasibility study
3.4.1 Technical feasibility
The project is technically feasible using common open-source tools. Python and its data-analysis and machine-learning ecosystem can support cleaning, feature engineering and experiments. A web framework, a relational DBMS and a browser interface can support the prototype. A standard development computer with approximately 8 GB RAM and 10 GB free storage is proposed; experiments will be limited to manageable data subsets if required. Deep-learning models will not be mandatory.
3.4.2 Economic and operational feasibility
Most proposed software is freely available for academic use. The anticipated budget is directed mainly to internet access, printing, stationery, local transport for requirements elicitation and final binding. Operationally, the application will reflect familiar activities such as checking stock, recording sales and reviewing reorders. Forecast assumptions will be visible, and a human user will retain authority over purchasing decisions.
3.4.3 Schedule feasibility
The proposed schedule spans eight months. Scope is controlled by limiting the model comparison to a baseline and a small number of candidate models, and by building a prototype rather than a production inventory platform.
3.5 Requirements elicitation and sampling
The target population comprises retail personnel who participate in stock, sales, procurement or store-management activities in selected retail businesses. Purposive sampling will recruit respondents with direct experience of inventory practices. A provisional planning target of 20–30 questionnaire participants is proposed, subject to accessible population, outlet permission and supervisor approval. A smaller subset may be interviewed to clarify workflows. The number approached, actual participation and usable responses will be documented in the final report; the planning target is not a claim about achieved sample size.
The structured questionnaire will capture respondent role, existing stock-management methods, stockout and overstock frequency, information used in reorder decisions, perceived value of forecasts and desired system outputs. Interviews will explore current workflow, trust and usability requirements. The instruments in the appendices will be reviewed by the supervisor before use. Permission will be obtained from participating outlets; participation will be voluntary, and no customer payment credentials or personally sensitive customer data will be requested.
3.6 Data sources and analysis
Questionnaire responses will be summarized using frequencies and percentages; selected numerical summaries may be reported where appropriate. Open-ended answers and interview notes will be grouped into themes such as stock monitoring, forecast trust, replenishment and usability. Only information relevant to system requirements will be retained in the project report.
The sales dataset will be inspected for schema, time coverage, missing values, duplicate rows, inconsistent identifiers, outliers and zero/intermittent demand. Preprocessing decisions will be documented. Candidate features may include lagged sales, rolling summaries, calendar indicators and price/promotional variables where available. All features will be computed from information available at the prediction point. Data will be split chronologically, with validation used for model tuning and a held-out period reserved for final comparison. Data leakage checks will be part of the experiment log.
3.7 System specification
The application will accept authorized user requests, product and stock information, sales transactions or imported historical records, and forecasting parameters. It will validate and store the information, generate a forecast for a defined product and horizon, combine forecast and inventory context, and return stock-risk indicators, suggested replenishment quantities, trends and reports. The application will show product, forecast date/horizon and assumptions with each recommendation.

3.13 Forecasting and replenishment logic
A baseline forecast will be compared with selected supervised models. Training and evaluation windows will preserve time order. Final selection will consider held-out error and computational practicality. For a product i and lead-time horizon L, the proposed target stock is the sum of forecast demand over the horizon plus an explicit safety-stock buffer: targetᵢ = Σₕ₌₁ᴸ forecastᵢ,ₕ + safety_stockᵢ. Net available stock is defined as on_handᵢ + confirmed_in_transitᵢ − allocatedᵢ, where allocation data are available. Suggested order quantity is max(0, targetᵢ − net_availableᵢ). If pack-size constraints are known, rounding will be documented; otherwise, the prototype will report the unrounded quantity. Overstock flags will use a disclosed threshold or upper target rather than an unstated universal rule.
The workflow shows how data validation, feature construction, chronological model comparison, forecast generation and inventory calculations connect. MAPE will be omitted or qualified for zero-demand observations. A forecast is not guaranteed future demand; the application will present it as an estimate for human review.

Figure 3.6: Forecasting and replenishment workflow
*MAPE will be used only where its denominator and interpretation are valid.
3.13.1 Proposed Replenishment Calculation and Assumptions
For product i, the target-stock quantity will be calculated as forecasted demand accumulated over the replenishment lead time, plus a configured safety-stock allowance. Net available inventory will be calculated from on-hand stock plus confirmed in-transit stock, less allocations where allocation data are available. The suggested replenishment quantity will be the non-negative difference between target stock and net available inventory, expressed as max(0, target stock − net available inventory). Where packaging constraints are known, the implementation may round the result to an available order pack and disclose the rounding rule. Overstock warnings will be generated against an explicitly configured upper target, and these rules will be evaluated using documented test scenarios rather than presented as universally optimal decisions.

CHAPTER 4: SYSTEM CODE GENERATION AND TESTING
4.1 Introduction
This chapter presents the planned approach for generating, integrating and evaluating the code for the proposed Smart Inventory Demand Forecasting and Optimization System. It describes the development environment, the intended implementation of the major modules, the integration of the forecasting service with inventory decision logic, and the verification and validation procedures. As this document is a proposal, the activities described in this chapter are planned rather than completed, and no test result or system outcome is presented as an observed finding. Actual screenshots, implementation decisions, measured model performance, test execution records and defects will replace the corresponding plans in the final report. The testing plan is designed to demonstrate whether the system satisfies the functional and non-functional specifications established in Chapter Three.
4.2 Planned System Code Generation
The system will be implemented incrementally in accordance with the iterative development approach described in Chapter Three. Initial work will establish the project repository, application configuration, relational database schema and authentication features. Subsequent increments will implement product maintenance, sales recording or import, stock monitoring and reporting. The machine learning component will then be developed and evaluated separately before being exposed to the application through a controlled service interface. Finally, forecast outputs will be integrated with inventory calculations to produce alerts, replenishment quantities and dashboard visualizations.
4.2.1 Proposed Development Environment
The development environment will use Python for preprocessing, feature engineering, forecasting experiments and the backend forecasting service. A suitable web framework will expose application functions and enforce input validation and authentication, while HTML, CSS and JavaScript or a suitable frontend framework will provide the browser interface. A relational DBMS, such as PostgreSQL or MySQL, will store users, products, transactions, inventory records and analytical outputs in normalized structures. Pandas, NumPy and selected machine learning libraries will support experimental evaluation of forecasting models. Git will track source changes and enable rollback of unsuccessful modifications, and all selected dependencies and versions will be documented in the final report.
4.2.2 Planned Application Modules
The authentication module will control access to protected features and prevent unauthorized modification of inventory data. Product and sales modules will maintain information required to interpret sales history and current stock conditions, with validation for missing fields, negative quantities and inconsistent product references. The forecasting module will retrieve prepared historical observations and return a forecast for a clearly identified product and horizon. The stock-risk module will compare predicted demand over a relevant replenishment horizon against available inventory and configurable assumptions. The replenishment module will calculate suggested order quantities, while the reporting dashboard will communicate historical demand, predictions, inventory status and the assumptions underlying recommendations.
4.2.3 Proposed Forecasting and Replenishment Logic
The forecasting experiment will include a transparent baseline and a small selection of supervised machine learning models chosen according to the available dataset and computational capacity. Historical observations will be partitioned chronologically, with any lag or rolling features calculated without using future information. Performance will be assessed using MAE and RMSE, and MAPE will be considered only for observations where zero or near-zero demand does not undermine interpretation. After comparative evaluation, the selected model will be saved and integrated into the forecasting service. The decision-support component will use the forecast, current stock and configurable lead-time or safety-stock assumptions to produce an explainable recommendation rather than an automatically executed purchase order.
4.3 Proposed Software Integration
Individual modules will initially be tested separately and then connected through documented application interfaces. The frontend will communicate with application services, which will retrieve and validate database records before requesting predictions from the forecasting component. Forecast results will be associated with the correct product, prediction date and forecast horizon to avoid using stale or mismatched predictions in inventory calculations. The stock-risk and replenishment modules will then consume forecast outputs and return formatted decision information to the dashboard. Integration tests will focus on both normal workflows and failure conditions, including missing sales history, database disconnection, invalid requests and unavailable prediction services.
4.4 Planned Testing Strategy
Testing will combine verification of the implementation against the specifications with validation of its usefulness to intended users. Unit tests will assess individual functions such as input validation, inventory adjustments, feature generation and reorder calculations. Integration and functional tests will exercise complete interactions among the interface, application services, database and forecasting module, while regression tests will be repeated after significant changes. Usability feedback will be collected from available peers and, where permission is obtained, retail personnel using a brief evaluation form. Performance, security and compatibility tests will examine response times under representative load, protected-route access and operation in supported browsers.
4.4.1 Forecasting Model Evaluation
The forecasting component will be evaluated on chronologically held-out retail observations that are not used to train the selected models. A baseline forecast will be included so that more complex models must demonstrate measurable improvement rather than being presumed superior. MAE and RMSE will be calculated on the same forecast horizon and comparable observations, and results will be reported by model and where appropriate by product group. MAPE will be reported only where its interpretation is reasonable, because zero-demand observations can make percentage errors undefined. The final report will document dataset selection, preprocessing choices, time partitions, tested parameters, numerical results and observed limitations.
4.4.2 Planned Verification and Validation Evidence
Each executed test will record its identifier, relevant system module, test condition, expected result, actual result and pass or fail status. Screenshots or logs will be retained as evidence for selected end-to-end tests and identified defects will be documented together with corrective action and retest outcomes. At least twenty test cases have been planned across the principal application modules in line with the departmental guide. The proposed cases are a starting point and will be refined after requirements validation and implementation details are finalized. All actual outcomes will remain blank until tests have been run on the working system.

Performance evaluation will record response times for selected operations under normal conditions and under a modest simulated concurrent workload representative of the project environment. The final report will present measured observations, relevant limitations and evidence of any corrections made after evaluation.

The first research objective will be evaluated through the documented exploratory analysis and identification of demand-related features in historical retail sales data. The second objective will be assessed using model comparison tables and held-out forecasting error measurements. CHAPTER 5: PROPOSED CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS FRAMEWORK
5.1 Introduction
This proposal has not yet generated implementation or evaluation results. This chapter therefore defines how conclusions and recommendations will be grounded in evidence after the system is built and tested; it does not claim that the objectives have already been achieved.
5.2 Anticipated contribution
The project is expected to produce an evaluated comparison of selected demand-forecasting approaches on a defined retail dataset and a working prototype that links forecasts to transparent stock-risk and replenishment suggestions. The contribution will be judged by both forecasting performance and whether the integrated application satisfies its stated functional requirements. The prototype will make its assumptions visible and preserve human authority over procurement.
5.3 Basis for conclusions
Demand analysis conclusions will be supported by documented data quality checks, descriptive analysis and selected forecasting features.
Forecasting conclusions will be supported by held-out chronological metrics compared with a baseline and interpreted in light of product-level variation.
Inventory-decision conclusions will be supported by tested scenarios for stockout risk, excess stock, positive replenishment and zero-order conditions.
System-effectiveness conclusions will use executed functional and integration tests, measured response observations and usability feedback.
Claims about generalization or operational value will be limited by the dataset, evaluation horizon and absence of live deployment.
5.4 Anticipated limitations
A public international dataset may not capture the market conditions, customer behavior or operational practices of Kenyan retailers. Participating outlets may not provide transaction histories, leaving requirements elicitation and model experimentation dependent on different sources. Forecast performance may vary for intermittent-demand products, promotions or short histories. Replenishment outputs depend on lead time, in-transit stock, safety stock and allocation assumptions; these may not represent every real constraint. The final report will document which limitations materialized and how they affect interpretation.
5.5 Recommendation framework
Recommendations will follow observed results. If forecasting performance is promising on held-out data, subsequent work may validate it on locally collected sales records before operational adoption. If results are weak or uneven, the report will identify affected product groups and consider data or feature improvements rather than overstate performance. If inventory suggestions depend on uncertain lead times or safety-stock assumptions, additional supplier and service-level information should be obtained before real purchasing use. Usability changes will be linked to documented feedback and retest evidence. Future work may extend the model comparison, incorporate uncertainty estimates, test realistic lead times, or evaluate the application in a supervised pilot.
5.6 Chapter summary
The final conclusions will be based on documented data analysis, forecasting evidence, tested system behavior and user feedback. The recommendations will follow those findings and be qualified by the limits of the dataset and prototype setting. No unobserved result is asserted in this proposal.
